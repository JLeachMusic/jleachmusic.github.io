Introduction

Improvisation is the process of creating music in the moment, often alongside other musicians or a track, and is best achieved through a mix of intuition (feeling the music) and explicit intention. In order to improvise well, we have to develop a strong intuitive sense with everything from our ear to our muscle memory, as well as a well-founded understanding of the music we're interacting with and creating. To develop these things, we need to improvise not only often, but also very intentionally. Mindless random noodling can be very useful for muscle memory and fluid movement on the instrument, but won't necessarily translate to anything interesting musically.

We can start the process of strengthening our ear by improvising while actively listening to the notes we play and how they interact with the music we're playing over (if not played in isolation), while being very mindful of the concepts we're currently working on.

Beginner improv

The first steps to improv will be taking the notes of your major scale and randomly traversing it over a track. Choose your root and scale, say D major, and find a song or backing track in that key. To find a song or track in a particular key, search for "songs in X major" or "X major backing track" and choose one you like*.

Once you've selected the key and track, play over it multiple times, starting with step one and moving down the list as you get comfortable with the process.

  • First playthrough: move randomly between the notes.
  • Second: pay very close attention to the notes you're playing, keeping track of the scale degrees while active listening.
  • Third: try to predict what notes will sound like before you play them, and remember what they sound like once you move away.

Once you've gotten comfortable with each of these steps, you'll be able to spend more time both analyzing and feeling the music you're creating. You can spend more time noticing which notes are more consonant ("good" sounding, harmonizing without much tension) and which are more dissonant ("bad" sounding, a lot of tension). From here on out, all of your improv should incorporate active listening and careful attention to the notes you play.

*some songs may be difficult for you to play over due to key changes or scales (other than major) you're not familiar with yet; simply set them aside for now and find one that's easier to play over.

Conclusion

Improvising in this manner is an extremely important first step; to eventually be able to express ourselves in sublime ways musically; to create music that reflects any emotion we choose; to intrigue both ourselves and anyone listening. Remember that it is a cumulation of skill and muscle memory, which will take time and experience to fully bloom into something meaningful. It's also crucial to not only play in context with a song, backing track, or other musicians, but also entirely on your own with the only music playing coming from yourself in the moment.

A few questions to ask yourself after improvising:

  1. Did you notice how some notes sounded consonant and others dissonant?
  2. Did you notice that sometimes a particular note would sound consonant at some points and dissonant at others?
  3. Did any in particular tend to stand out as repeatedly consonant or dissonant?
  4. Did you move quickly between notes often, or did you usually let them ring out for a while?
  5. How well were you able to keep track of degrees while you played?
  6. How well were you able to predict notes before you heard them in the beginning, versus near the end of your practice?
  7. Were there any moments in your improvising that stood out to you as impressive, and how did that make you feel?

Goals

  • be able to keep track of degrees consistently, both in remembering what you've already played and in finding the next one quickly.
  • see how much your ear's predictive ability improves over time. it may be a very slow process but you will notice it with time.

Next steps

Repeat this process of playing over songs you enjoy. There is no point at which you cannot have a new idea, even if you've played over the same song a hundred times. Any difficulty we encounter in creating fresh ideas while playing can often be overcome with careful analysis of how we're playing and attempting to incorporate new theory or technique into it.

Every now and then, record yourself playing. Listen back and critique it modestly. Do you hear a spot where you think you should have played a different note? Do you think your technique on a particular part of the melody could be improved? What would you have done differently if you could be in that exact moment again?

Once you feel comfortable with the process, feel free to move on to The Major Scale: Lesson #2.

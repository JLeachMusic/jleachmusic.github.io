Scales in music are an extremely essential part of our shared musical tradition, informing the creation and understanding of the art for millennia. They are essentially a collection of notes following some sort of pattern, typically an "interval pattern" or a pattern of whole-steps and half-steps. This pattern is applied to a special note called the root, and just like the roots of a tree, so too grows the scale based on the pattern.

Broadly speaking, there are two main uses for scales:

  1. for the composition (creation) of new music, whether by improvising or by using it to write music.
  2. for the analysis (understanding/explaining) of music and musical objects.

In our Western tradition of music, the major scale is of the highest importance to us. While any scale could be used in the previously mentioned analytical or compositional ways, it's simply by tradition and convention that the major scale takes such precedence.

Scale Patterns

The major scale uses a pattern of whole-steps (w) and half-steps (h) as follows: wwh w wwh (spaces included for readability). If we start on C for example, the notes would be as follows: C D E F G A B C. The major scale has seven notes, and even though C appears twice in the list, it is not counted as a separate note because the final step in the pattern is simply where the pattern lands back on the root, repeating into infinity. Eg., C D E F G A B C D E F G A B C D E....

It's important that the names of the notes are in alphabetical order, and neither repeat any letters nor skip any. For example, in the A major scale, the notes are as such: A B C# D E F# G#, where the next note is always the next letter. In another example, the F major scale would be written as F G A B♭ C D E. This convention is why accidentals (the notes between the naturals) can have two names, so that we can maintain alphabetical order and eliminate the possiblity of confusion if, for some reason, we had the notes A A# C written in our scale, as A B♭ C is considerably clearer.

Degrees

When we start on the root note and ascend the scale, we can count the notes starting with "one" for the root, and ending on "seven" for the final note, continuing on with "eight" for the octave (oct- = eight) and so on. So the notes of the C major scale would be counted as such:

1. C
2. D
3. E
4. F
5. G
6. A
7. B

These numbers are called scale degrees, and are extremely important for understanding melody and harmony alike in music. We can refer to a note as being the nth degree of some scale; eg., F is the 4th degree of C major, or G being the 2nd degree of F major. Much of more advanced theory relies on understanding scale degrees and being able to articulate concepts using them to other musicians. It's a simple system of literally counting the notes, but has major consequences overall in our tradition. The term diatonic refers to the major scale; notes that fall in the scale are called diatonic notes, and non-diatonic applies to those that are not.

Other scales

The power of scales is that they can be built from any interval pattern we want. An example would be the minor scale, made from the pattern whw whw w, or the phrygian scale, being hww w hww. We can also do distances greater than a whole-step, such as three half-steps (a "step-and-a-half"). If we write it as 3 to denote three half-steps, we can use it to create a scale such as the major pentatonic scale: ww3 w3.

Note that not all scales have seven notes like the major scale. The major scale is heptatonic, from hepta- meaning seven, and -tonic relating to notes. Other scales could be pentatonic with five notes, or hexatonic with six; essentially any amount of notes can be used in a scale with the right pattern. Having more or less than seven notes also means we will sometimes have to repeat or skip letters, breaking our earlier rule of alphabetical order, but it is unavoidable in those cases.

Conclusion

A skilled musician will find effectively infinite use in scales, whether in creating or communicating/understanding musical concepts and relationships, or in composing or improvising. They are incredibly effective in being used in technical exercises, ear training, theory, composition, and so much more. Be patient and take the time to take in and wholly understand the principles of scales and how they can be applied to your musical journey.

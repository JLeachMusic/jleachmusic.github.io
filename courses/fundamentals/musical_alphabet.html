In our Western tradition of music, there are twelve total notes. They're named after the letters of the English alphabet, starting on A and ending with G. Seven of them are what we call "natural", or are just the letters by themselves; the remaining five are called "accidentals", and are the "sharps and flats" that fall between the naturals.

Half-steps and Whole-steps

A half-step is what we call the distance from one note to the very next one, whether up or down, and just as "two halves make a whole", so too do "two half-steps make a whole-step". Therefore, if a distance between notes is two half-steps away from each other, it's called a whole-step.

Natural notes

Below are the natural notes, with the accidentals between them represented as dots:

A . B C . D . E F . G . A

Notice how not all of the naturals have an accidental between them (eg., B -> C and E -> F). These notes are a half-step away from one another rather than a whole-step. Because of this, it can be thought of in terms of whole-steps and half-steps, where we use the following pattern, starting on A: whole half whole whole half whole whole, or whw whw w. From A to B is a whole-step, from B to C is a half, from C to D is a whole, etc.

Accidentals

Accidentals, also known as the sharps/flats, or the "black keys" if you're on piano, are the notes that fall between the naturals. They each have two names, based on the notes around them. Since it is a half-step higher than one neighbor and a half-step lower than the other, it is named sharp or flat relative to those neighbors. For example, the note that falls between A and B is called A# (A sharp) or B♭ (B flat) because it is sharper than A, and flatter than B. This is the case for all of the accidentals.

The Chromatic Scale

Scales can seem like a somewhat complex topic in music, but in this case, they can simply be thought of as a collection of notes. The chromatic scale then is simply the term we use to refer to the group of all twelve notes. When something is "chromatic", it's making movements in half-steps upwards or downwards, such as a melody that does something like A -> A# -> B -> C. When you hear the term "chromatic", understand that it relates to the fact that the structure in the music you're studying is going up or down in half-steps in some manner. If you think of chromatic in terms of a rainbow, consider the idea of it being like the collection of all of the colors in the spectrum.

Octaves

An octave is a note that is twelve half-steps higher or lower than another note, and will always be the same note name. For example, starting on A and going up twelve half-steps will lead you to another A, but an octave higher, and the reverse is true as well. The notes can be raised or lowered into infinity, and still maintain their names. Understanding that the names of the notes are essentially a circle that just restarts after twelve half-steps is crucial to understanding music overall. All notes have octaves reaching into infinity. Start on any note of your choosing, move up twelve half-steps, and you'll find that same note again. It does sound different because it's higher or lower, but if you play both of the notes simultaneously, you'll quickly hear that they are indistinguishable from one another, because they are truly the same note.

Intervals are the distances between notes. The first intervals you learn in music are the half-step or the distance from one note to the very next one, and the whole-step or the equivalent of two half-steps ("two halves make a whole").

Naming Convention

According to the convention we use, intervals are named after the distances between notes in the major scale from the root. The distance from 1 (first degree) to 2 (second degree) in the scale is a whole-step (two half-steps), but in our "scale interval" convention, is called a second. Following from that, from 1 to 3 is a third, 1 to 4 is a fourth, etc. The distances from the root are displayed below with their interval name and the distance in half-steps:

degree half-steps interval name
1 0 unison
2 2 second
3 4 third
4 5 fourth
5 7 fifth
6 9 sixth
7 11 seventh
8 12 octave

These interval names can and do often go past the octave, reaching 9th, 10th, etc., but are simply the same notes in higher octaves. Notice in the above table that a unison is the distance from a note to itself, which is simply no distance at all. If two musicians play the same note in the same octave simultaneously, then those notes are in unison.

It's also important to realize the difference between saying "a third" and "the third"; the former refers to an interval of four half-steps, but the latter would refer to the actual third degree of the major scale. For example, in the key of A, "the third" is the note C#, but we would say that "G# is a third above E."

Major and Perfect Intervals

The major scale allows us seven basic intervals (eight if we include the octave), but there are twelve total notes, and being limited to those seven would severely limit our ability to explain and interpret music. As such, the basic intervals are called either major or perfect, as listed below:

third ✓
fourth ✓
fifth ✓
sixth ✓
seventh ✓

These prefixes are called the quality of a note, so the quality of a "major third" is "major", and "perfect" is the quality of a "perfect fifth". The octave is just the same note as the root, so it will always be perfect, and intervals higher than the root-octave will always be the same quality as the first octave's note; eg., a 9th (the distance an octave higher than a 2nd) will be major, just the same as a 2nd.

Non-Diatonic Interval Qualities

Intervals are named after the major scale, but are limited to just those seven (eight including octave) intervals. As we're missing intervals for the other 5 notes in the chromatic scale, we can simply modify the qualities of the intervals on either side to name the new ones.

The way we name non-diatonic interval (intervals that fall outside of the major scale) qualities is based on the interval we're modifying, and its base quality. If an interval is perfect, then sharpening it would make it augmented, and flattening it would make it diminished. For a major interval, sharpening also makes it augmented, but flattening makes it minor. As such, the interval between a fifth and a sixth could be called either an augmented fifth or a minor sixth, depending on context that we won't go into here. Notice the similarity this has to how we name accidentals based on the natural notes surrounding them.
